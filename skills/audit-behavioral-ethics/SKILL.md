# Audit Behavioral Ethics

Evaluate whether a behavioral design helps affected people pursue outcomes they value while preserving informed agency. Do not reduce ethics to legal compliance or designer intent.

## Define the intervention and stakeholders

Record:

- target population and vulnerable subgroups;
- target behavior and discouraged alternatives;
- claimed user benefit and measurable user outcome;
- business, institutional, or political objective;
- mechanisms used to influence behavior;
- who chooses, benefits, pays, supplies data, and bears risk;
- whether the intervention is voluntary, reversible, and understandable.
- what the organization's incentives and success metrics reward in practice;
- whether personalization, timing, or inferred knowledge may feel unexpected or invasive.

Audit the actual experience, including defaults, copy, timing, visual hierarchy, friction, notifications, data use, cancellation, and failure states.

## Apply bright-line checks

Treat these as presumptively unacceptable:

- deception or material omission;
- false scarcity, false social proof, or disguised advertising;
- non-consensual enrollment in paid, privacy-invasive, or risky options;
- obstruction of cancellation, refund, deletion, consent withdrawal, or benefit access;
- coercion through essential services, power imbalance, or retaliation;
- exploiting distress, loneliness, fear, children, addiction, or impaired capacity for unrelated engagement or revenue;
- variable rewards, infinite consumption, or punitive streaks designed primarily to maximize time or spend;
- experiments that conceal material risk or lack appropriate oversight.

A bright-line failure requires redesign, not cosmetic disclosure.

## Audit eight dimensions

Read [audit-checklist.md](references/audit-checklist.md). Evaluate:

1. User benefit by the person's own lights
2. Autonomy and meaningful choice
3. Transparency and comprehension
4. Friction symmetry, reversal, and exit
5. Habit, compulsion, and emotional exploitation
6. Privacy, data proportionality, and personalization
7. Distributional effects and vulnerability
8. Incentives, governance, measurement, and accountability

Audit both payoff and signal: an incentive can change behavior while also communicating that quality, honesty, teamwork, or user welfare is secondary. Check whether the intervention would still seem acceptable if its targeting logic and sponsor objective were plainly explained.

Consider both the experiencing self during use and the remembering self evaluating the episode later. Good retrospective ratings do not erase prolonged harm, and a pleasant moment does not prove long-term benefit.

## Assign risk and remediation

Read [risk-rubric.md](references/risk-rubric.md). Rate each issue by severity, reach, likelihood, reversibility, power imbalance, and evidence. Use one overall level:

- Low: aligned and readily reversible
- Moderate: correctable ambiguity or limited risk
- High: material manipulation or harm requiring redesign and review
- Critical: coercive, deceptive, exploitative, or dangerous; do not ship

Use this remediation order:

1. Remove the harmful mechanism.
2. Realign incentives and success metrics.
3. Change defaults, friction, timing, or eligibility.
4. Add meaningful control, reversal, and recovery.
5. Improve disclosure and comprehension.
6. Add monitoring, independent review, and stop conditions.

Disclosure alone does not cure coercion, deception, or obstructive friction.

## Deliver the audit

Use the user's language and provide:

1. Executive verdict and overall risk
2. Intended benefit and stakeholder conflicts
3. Findings table: mechanic, affected group, mechanism, harm, severity, evidence
4. Bright-line failures
5. Required changes before launch
6. Safer alternatives
7. Guardrail and welfare metrics
8. Governance, review owner, and stop conditions
9. Residual uncertainty

Be direct. Do not hide a high-risk finding behind a balanced list of minor positives.
