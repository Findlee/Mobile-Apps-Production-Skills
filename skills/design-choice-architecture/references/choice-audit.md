# Choice Architecture Audit

## 1. Classify the decision

Record stakes, reversibility, frequency, familiarity, delayed consequences, feedback quality, option complexity, emotional load, and time pressure.

Guidance is especially valuable when attention is scarce, the decision is difficult or rare, feedback is delayed, or option attributes do not map clearly to lived outcomes.

## 2. Map stakeholders and incentives

Ask:

- Who chooses?
- Who uses or experiences the result?
- Who pays?
- Who profits?
- Who holds information?
- Who bears delayed risk?

Misalignment is a design constraint, not a copywriting problem.

## 4. Trace plausible paths

Do not assume choosers inspect every option and attribute. Trace the likely sequence of attention and comparison:

1. What is noticed first?
2. Which options enter the consideration set?
3. Does the chooser compare within an option, across one attribute, or by a shortcut?
4. Which path becomes sticky after the first action?
5. Does that path improve both fluency and accuracy?

Test paths through observation, click or eye sequence where available, think-aloud, and decision simulations. Preference ratings after the fact may rationalize the architecture rather than reveal the path.

## 5. Test comprehension and accuracy

Ask a representative chooser to explain:

- what each option does;
- total cost and material trade-offs;
- what happens if they do nothing;
- how to reverse the decision;
- likely lived consequences;
- why an option was recommended.

Recognition of labels is not comprehension.

When a correct or fitting option can be defined, give representative users a realistic goal and assess whether the architecture helps them find it. Separate a fast, pleasant choice from an accurate one.

## 6. Detect sludge and dark patterns

Flag asymmetric steps, repeated entry, delay, hidden alternatives, confusing toggles, inaccessible language, drip pricing, prechecked consent, visual interference, forced continuity, confirmshaming, and unnecessary support contact.

Classify friction as:

- protective and proportionate;
- operational but reducible;
- accidental sludge;
- intentional obstruction or manipulation.

## 7. Compare architectures

For each proposed architecture, state:

- who benefits and who may be harmed;
- assumptions about preferences;
- default rationale;
- likely error and regret modes;
- data required for personalization;
- how easily the choice can be changed;
- what evidence would make another architecture preferable.
