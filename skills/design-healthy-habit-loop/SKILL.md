# Design Healthy Habit Loop

Create repetition only when repetition itself helps the person obtain continuing value. A habit is not automatically the right product goal.

Treat habit as a learned context-response association strengthened by rewarded repetition in a stable context. Motivation can initiate action, but stable cues, low friction, repetition, and experienced reward make it more automatic.

## Pass the habit gate

Before designing a loop, confirm:

- the behavior must recur to produce value;
- a natural context or recurring need exists;
- the expected frequency is realistic;
- the person wants the outcome;
- automation would not serve the person better;
- failure to engage does not justify manufactured anxiety;
- the behavior is not a rare, high-stakes decision that deserves deliberation.

If the gate fails, design a one-time action, reminder, checklist, service, or choice architecture instead.

## Select the behavior

Turn the aspiration into multiple candidate behaviors. Choose a behavior that:

- materially supports the outcome;
- the person wants to do;
- is feasible on a difficult but realistic day.

Shrink it to a starter step or minimum viable version. Do not raise the success threshold merely because the person sometimes does more.

## Design the immediate behavior

Read [habit-loop-patterns.md](references/habit-loop-patterns.md).

Use B=MAP at one moment:

1. **Prompt:** tie the behavior to an existing routine, event, place, state, or user-chosen reminder.
2. **Ability:** reduce time, money, physical effort, mental effort, unfamiliarity, and disruption to routine.
3. **Motivation:** rely on an already valued aspiration; do not make volatile motivation carry a difficult action.

Use the recipe: **After [specific anchor], I will [tiny behavior].** Make the anchor precise enough to rehearse.

Design the surrounding context, not only the prompt. Remove friction from the beneficial action, add proportionate friction to the competing action, and keep the cue-response setting sufficiently stable for learning.

## Reinforce without compulsion

Create immediate success feedback that is authentic and proportional. Prefer competence, progress, completion, relief, meaning, or social support over extrinsic pressure.

Distinguish the reward that initially makes repetition worthwhile from the cue-response association that later supports automaticity. Do not assume a larger extrinsic reward creates a stronger or healthier habit.

If using a product loop, specify:

- internal or situational need;
- external trigger, if needed initially;
- smallest action that anticipates value;
- reward that actually resolves or advances the need;
- optional investment that stores user value or prepares the next useful action;
- how external prompting fades as the routine becomes stable.

Variable rewards are optional, not mandatory. Never use uncertainty merely to extend sessions or exploit distress.

## Troubleshoot in a fixed order

When the behavior fails:

1. Check whether the prompt occurred and was understood.
2. Make the action easier.
3. Confirm the chosen behavior is genuinely wanted.
4. Adjust the anchor and rehearse.
5. Replace the behavior rather than blaming the person.

Use life or context changes as habit-discontinuity windows: old cues may temporarily lose control, making redesign easier. Do not assume a habit transfers automatically to a new device, location, schedule, or social environment.

Do not default to more notifications, guilt, public commitment, punishment, or larger rewards.

## Apply safety requirements

Read [habit-safety.md](references/habit-safety.md) for product loops. Always preserve pause, snooze, exit, and recovery after interruption. Do not punish broken streaks or imply moral failure.

Measure:

- real-world user outcome;
- successful repetitions and time to value;
- prompt dependence;
- voluntary return and easy disengagement;
- regret, anxiety, complaints, sleep disruption, and compulsive use;
- unequal effects on vulnerable groups.

## Deliver the habit specification

Use the user's language and include:

1. Habit-gate decision
2. User aspiration and target behavior
3. Anchor, tiny behavior, and success feedback
4. B=MAP diagnosis
5. Context, friction, repetition, and reward plan
6. Product loop, if relevant
7. Troubleshooting rules
8. Safety guardrails
9. Outcome, habit-health, and harm metrics
10. Experiment plan
