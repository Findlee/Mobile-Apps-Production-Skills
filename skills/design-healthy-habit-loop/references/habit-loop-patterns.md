# Healthy Habit Loop Patterns

This reference combines behavior design and healthy product loops with the context, repetition, and reward foundations of habit formation, constrained by user welfare and autonomy.

## Select a Golden Behavior

Generate many concrete behaviors before choosing. Select one that scores well on:

- **Impact:** likely to advance the aspiration
- **Motivation:** the person already wants to do it
- **Ability:** feasible in the actual context

Do not select a behavior only because it is measurable or monetizable.

## Make it tiny

Use either:

- **Starter step:** begin the larger behavior, such as opening the plan
- **Scale back:** perform a very small complete version, such as reviewing one item

The tiny version remains a success even when the person sometimes does more.

## Create a precise recipe

```text
After [existing routine or reliable event], I will [tiny behavior].
```

Good anchors are specific, occur at the right frequency and location, and end immediately before the new behavior can start.

## Diagnose ability

Find the weakest link:

- time;
- money;
- physical effort;
- mental effort;
- routine fit;
- social acceptability or access where relevant.

Change the environment or behavior to strengthen the weakest link.

## Reinforce success

Use immediate positive emotion or feedback that feels authentic to the person. Reinforce the act of showing up, not only a large outcome. Avoid praise that infantilizes, compares unfairly, or creates pressure.

## Shape context, repetition, and friction

- Keep the relevant cue and response close in time and stable enough to learn.
- Repeat in the real context where automatic action should occur, not only in a training screen.
- Remove distance, steps, preparation, and decision friction from the beneficial response.
- Add small, proportionate friction to the competing response when the person wants help resisting it.
- Arrange tools, defaults, and social surroundings so the intended action is the path of least resistance.
- Track automaticity and prompt dependence separately from repetition count.

When location, schedule, device, household, job, or routine changes, treat it as a habit-discontinuity window. Re-establish the cue and tiny response deliberately; do not interpret disruption as lost motivation.

## Product loop

### Trigger

Start with a user-chosen external trigger near the natural opportunity. Over time, the situation or need may become sufficient. Negative emotion can be a real internal trigger, but exploiting it for unrelated engagement is unsafe.

### Action

Offer the smallest action in anticipation of value. Minimize steps to the first useful outcome.

### Reward

Resolve or advance the underlying need. Possible value types include social connection, useful information or resources, and mastery or completion. Variability is not required.

### Investment

Ask for a small, optional contribution only after value is delivered. The investment should store value for the person: preferences, data, content, relationships, reputation, or skill. It may prepare the next useful trigger.

## Troubleshooting

Use this order:

1. Did the prompt happen?
2. Was the action easy enough at that moment?
3. Is the behavior a good match for the person?
4. Is the anchor precise and rehearsed?
5. Is feedback immediate and authentic?
6. Does the loop deliver real value each cycle?
7. Did a context change remove or replace the learned cue?

Iterate the design; do not diagnose weak character or willpower.
