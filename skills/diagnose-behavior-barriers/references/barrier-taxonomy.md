# Behavioral Barrier Taxonomy

Use this taxonomy to organize evidence. Do not treat it as a checklist of explanations that must all appear.

## COM-B: system conditions for behavior

### Capability

- **Physical:** strength, stamina, dexterity, motor skill, or physical competence.
- **Psychological:** knowledge, comprehension, memory, attention, reasoning, or psychological skill.

### Opportunity

- **Physical:** time, resources, access, location, workflow, prompts, and environmental affordances.
- **Social:** norms, roles, relationships, culture, status, and interpersonal support or pressure.

### Motivation

- **Reflective:** goals, plans, beliefs, evaluations, identity, and conscious intention.
- **Automatic:** emotion, impulse, desire, learned association, and habit.

Capability and opportunity can change motivation; behavior can also change future capability, opportunity, and motivation. Diagnose the competing behavior with the same model.

When more detail is necessary, use TDF domains as prompts: knowledge; skills; social or professional role and identity; beliefs about capabilities; optimism; beliefs about consequences; reinforcement; intentions; goals; memory, attention, and decision processes; environmental context and resources; social influences; emotion; and behavioral regulation.

## CREATE: six gates for the behavioral path

### Cue

The actor does not notice, remember, or recognize an opportunity to act.

Signals: low exposure, missed reminders, wrong channel, low salience, competing attention, no implementation plan.

### Reaction

The immediate automatic response is aversion, distrust, fear, confusion, boredom, or social threat.

Signals: rapid dismissal, negative affect, visual or verbal associations, identity mismatch, distrust of sender. Do not infer an emotion without user evidence.

### Evaluation

The actor does not expect benefits to exceed costs or substitutes an easier judgment for the actual decision.

Signals: unclear value, hidden future benefit, salient loss, bad comparison, uncertainty, weak mapping from choice to outcome, competing alternative looks better.

### Ability

The actor lacks resources, knowledge, confidence, access, or a feasible path.

Check time, money, physical effort, mental effort, routine disruption, social acceptability, credentials, devices, permissions, accessibility, and offline dependencies.

### Timing

The actor can act but not at this moment. Urgent competing goals dominate or benefits are too delayed.

Signals: repeated deferral, prompt arrives outside opportunity, no deadline or implementation intention, action requires coordination, energy or attention is depleted.

### Experience

Prior experience with the product, action, institution, or category creates avoidance or pessimism.

Signals: prior failure, unresolved error, broken trust, learned helplessness, support history, stigma, inconsistent past reward.

For automatic behavior, prioritize Cue, Reaction, and Ability; deliberate evaluation may be bypassed.

## B=MAP: immediate action check

A behavior occurs when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt converge at the same moment.

- **Prompt:** Did a specific, actionable signal occur?
- **Ability:** Was the behavior easy enough in that moment?
- **Motivation:** Did the actor already value the outcome enough for the remaining difficulty?

Troubleshoot in the order Prompt -> Ability -> behavior match or Motivation. Increasing motivation while leaving the action difficult is usually fragile.

## Additional lenses

### Competing behavior

The actor is never a blank slate. Identify the default, habit, workaround, or more immediately rewarding action that currently wins.

### Friction and sludge

Inventory steps, decisions, waiting, repeated entry, search, uncertainty, coordination, inaccessible language, and recovery. Determine whether friction protects the actor or blocks an outcome they want.

### Automatic versus deliberative

Automatic paths are cue- and context-sensitive. Deliberative paths are more vulnerable to cognitive load, unclear mappings, anchoring, framing, and choice complexity. Do not force deliberation into every routine action or automaticity into consequential decisions.

### Decision-quality minefields

Consider a cognitive mechanism only when relevant evidence exists:

- anchor dominates an estimate;
- vivid examples displace base rates;
- similarity displaces probability;
- an easier question replaces the real one;
- only available evidence is considered;
- a coherent story creates overconfidence;
- losses or reference points distort comparison;
- framing reverses preference;
- the inside view ignores comparable cases.
