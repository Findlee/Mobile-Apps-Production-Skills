# Operate Product Team

Create a practical operating system in which a durable cross-functional team owns outcomes, learns before overinvesting, and delivers reliable changes.

## Orchestration safety

This skill may run directly or as a bounded specialist for `product-manager` or `product-review`. When invoked by a parent:

- answer only the team-operating question and reuse its evidence, commitments, and constraints;
- do not re-enter the parent or create a competing product backlog;
- separate product defects from operating-model causes;
- return strategy, research, experiment, scope, specification, metrics, UX, engineering, or leadership dependencies as handoff recommendations;
- stop when the operating uncertainty is resolved enough for the parent's decision.

Only one orchestrator owns final synthesis and priority.

## Core rules

1. Optimize for customer and business outcomes, not feature volume or ritual compliance.
2. Give the team a meaningful problem, strategic context, constraints, and success measure—not an answer disguised as a requirement.
3. Keep discovery and delivery within one durable team.
4. Product, design, and engineering collaborate from problem framing through measurement.
5. Collaboration is neither consensus, democracy, waterfall handoff, nor lowest-common-denominator compromise.
6. Use evidence to update decisions while preserving accountable judgment.
7. Keep goals, ideas, evidence/delivery steps, tasks, and fixed commitments distinct.
8. Limit work in progress until steps and releases reach analyzed outcomes.
9. Instrument, monitor, support, and learn from every meaningful release.
10. Adapt principles and cadence to organization size, risk, product type, and constraints.
11. Assess majority behavior and material exceptions; do not demand theatrical perfection.
12. Change organizations through bounded pilots and demonstrated outcomes when broad change would create avoidable disruption.
13. Do not rename feature teams “empowered” while stakeholders prescribe solutions and teams lack authority, access, or competence.
14. Never invent organizational evidence, commitment flexibility, capacity, targets, timelines, or authority. Label planning numbers provisional unless supplied or authorized.

## Numeric discipline

Before presenting a numeric plan, separate user-supplied constraints from reviewer proposals. For every unsourced sample size, meeting length, cadence, capacity percentage, pilot size, duration, quota, or target, either omit the number or label it `Provisional proposal` at its first occurrence and state who must calibrate it from which evidence. A roadmap heading or schedule does not make a number evidence-backed.

## Workflow

### 1. Establish scope and authority

Identify team/unit, product area, requested change and owner, commitments and constraints, product/business/transformation outcomes, and actions requiring executive, people, budget, structural, contractual, or policy authority. For assessment-only work, diagnose and recommend without changing live organization or process.

### 2. Assess the operating pattern

Inspect evidence across team durability, delivery/feature/product behavior, product/design/engineering/data/research/leadership competence, customer/data/market access, strategy/problem selection, discovery breadth/risk coverage, release/monitor/support/rollback, trust/ownership/coaching, stakeholder requests, commitments, approvals, and surprises.

Use [assessment-and-transition.md](references/assessment-and-transition.md). Separate observed practice from title, documented process, and aspiration.

### 3. Define the team charter

Specify ownership area, customers/business context, outcome/problem, value/viability/usability/feasibility/ethics/delivery/operation responsibility, boundaries/dependencies/shared services, decision authority/escalation, cadence, and expected evidence. Do not hold a team accountable for an outcome it cannot influence.

### 4. Establish cross-functional roles

Cover accountabilities regardless of titles:

- **product:** value, viability, customer/business/data context, outcome;
- **design:** complete experience, usability, research synthesis, interaction quality;
- **engineering/tech lead:** feasibility, enabling technology, delivery, security, reliability, operations;
- **shared:** discovery, ethics, evidence, prioritization, learning.

Add domain, data, research, legal, clinical, operations, marketing, sales, support, or security expertise when required. Define who decides when evidence cannot resolve disagreement and how material dissent is recorded.

### 5. Connect goals, ideas, steps, and tasks

Use:

- **Goals:** measurable customer, business, quality, or technical outcomes.
- **Ideas:** competing hypothetical ways to affect goals.
- **Evidence/delivery steps:** next activities that increase confidence or deliver bounded value.
- **Tasks:** implementation work linked to a step.

Keep the wider idea bank outside active work. A step is complete only after results are analyzed and the decision updates. Read [team-cadence-and-artifacts.md](references/team-cadence-and-artifacts.md).

### 6. Integrate discovery and delivery

For every active idea: identify assumptions/risks, choose a credible evidence step, include builders/operators, update direction from evidence, deliver the smallest complete increment at proportionate confidence, instrument/monitor, and feed evidence into the next decision.

Discovery is not a detached lab, design phase, or refinement ceremony. Delivery includes outcome, support, reliability, and post-deploy learning.

### 7. Create the cadence

Use the minimum cadence that maintains shared context: customer/market contact, collaborative discovery, outcome/idea/step review, delivery planning linked to steps, small verified releases, strategy/metric review, stakeholder communication before surprises, and retrospectives that change behavior. Remove meetings that produce no decision, evidence, coordination, coaching, or risk reduction.

### 8. Partner with stakeholders

For each request uncover the underlying problem/outcome, expose fixed commitments and sources, share options/evidence/risk/trade-offs early, clarify rights/escalation, explain rejected ideas, and avoid promising a solution before discovery unless genuinely fixed. Trust depends on competence, transparency, no surprises, and results—not accepting every request.

### 9. Manage commitments

Audit contractual, regulatory, customer, operational, and executive obligations. Reduce, renegotiate, reinterpret, or separate them only where authorized. Keep remaining commitments visible; do not relabel them as discovery. Protect explicit learning/improvement capacity, but label any suggested percentage provisional until owners reconcile real capacity and commitments.

### 10. Transition through a pilot

For broad change, choose a bounded pilot with willing participants, cross-functional competence, meaningful work, supportive leadership, and credible stakeholders. Define outcome, baseline, rights, coaching, risks, review points, and expand/stop conditions. Expand only after local evidence; do not impose organization-wide ceremony before competence/context exist.

### 11. Inspect and adapt

Track outcome movement, time to evidence/value, ideas tested versus built, release quality/recovery, customer/data access, decision latency/dependencies, stakeholder trust/surprises, ownership/coaching/load, and whether learning changes plans. Treat the operating model itself as a hypothesis.

## Output contract

Start with a short **Numeric provenance** note that separates numbers supplied by the user from provisional recommendations. If the plan subdivides a supplied horizon into phases, label the allocation provisional and name who must calibrate it. Do not hide provisional numbers inside tables or phase headings.

Deliver:

1. scope, authority, and transformation outcome;
2. evidence-based current-state assessment;
3. target charter, roles, and decision rights;
4. goal/idea/step/task model;
5. discovery-delivery and release workflow;
6. cadence and stakeholder collaboration;
7. commitments, dependencies, risks, and coaching needs;
8. pilot/transition plan with measures and review points.

Use [team-output.md](references/team-output.md).

## Completion gate

- The team owns a meaningful problem and can influence its outcome.
- Required competence and decision rights are present or gaps are explicit.
- Product, design, and engineering collaborate before commitment.
- Goals, ideas, steps, tasks, and commitments are separate.
- Discovery considers alternatives and material risks.
- Releases are instrumented, monitored, and supportable.
- Stakeholders have collaboration and escalation paths.
- Cadence produces decisions/evidence rather than process theater.
- Transition scope matches authority and readiness.
