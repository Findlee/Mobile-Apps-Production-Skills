# Problem framing

Use this reference to avoid solving a precise but incorrect problem.

## Separate four layers

- **Observed situation:** what people currently do or what evidence shows.
- **Need or outcome:** what must become possible or better.
- **Constraint:** a real boundary such as safety, time, regulation, platform, or cost.
- **Proposed solution:** a feature, screen, automation, or pattern that may or may not address the need.

Do not restate a proposed feature as the user need.

## Find the underlying cause

Ask why the requested behavior or problem occurs, continuing while the answer reveals a deeper actionable cause. Do not perform “Five Whys” mechanically or assume one root cause. Complex systems often have several contributing causes.

Check:

- what triggers the situation;
- what the user is trying to accomplish outside the interface;
- what workarounds exist;
- what knowledge or coordination is missing;
- which part of the burden belongs in the system;
- whether the issue is caused by policy, process, content, technology, or interface.

## Diverge before converging

Generate several plausible problem frames, including one that does not require the proposed feature. Then converge using evidence, consequence, strategic fit, and feasibility.

Example alternatives:

- help users perform the task;
- remove the need for the task;
- prevent the triggering problem;
- automate a safe part;
- improve coordination or visibility;
- change policy or content instead of adding UI.

## Problem statement checklist

A useful problem statement:

- names an actor or affected group;
- describes an outcome rather than a component;
- includes the important context and constraint;
- is broad enough for alternative solutions;
- is narrow enough to evaluate;
- does not invent motivation unsupported by evidence.

## Scope and success

Define:

- in-scope activity and boundary;
- desired user outcome;
- desired business outcome;
- unacceptable harms or tradeoffs;
- leading signals and final outcomes;
- what would indicate the problem was framed incorrectly.

Metrics must not replace qualitative understanding. A conversion increase can coexist with confusion, coercion, or long-term trust loss.
