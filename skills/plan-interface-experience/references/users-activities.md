# Users, activities, and context

Use this reference to model meaningful differences without inventing decorative personas.

## Evidence sources

Prefer, when available:

- direct observation of work or everyday behavior;
- interviews tied to concrete past events;
- support requests and failure reports;
- analytics and search logs interpreted with context;
- existing workflows, artifacts, and workarounds;
- accessibility and environmental constraints;
- stakeholder knowledge labeled by source and confidence.

Do not present generated examples as research data.

## Relevant user differences

Capture differences only when they affect the experience:

- novice, occasional, frequent, or expert use;
- domain knowledge and vocabulary;
- authority, ownership, and collaboration role;
- motor, visual, auditory, speech, cognitive, or situational access needs;
- motivation, urgency, stress, and consequence;
- device, input method, connectivity, location, and privacy;
- individual versus shared or supervised activity.

Avoid unsupported age, occupation, personality, or lifestyle detail.

## Activity hierarchy

Model from outcome to operation:

```text
Be-goal or identity outcome
└── Activity or job
    ├── Task
    │   └── Operation or interaction
    └── Task
```

Design for the activity so tasks work together coherently. Optimizing a local operation can damage the broader outcome.

## Activity questions

- What starts the activity?
- What does successful completion change in the real world?
- Which tools, people, or channels participate?
- What knowledge is in the user's head versus visible in the environment?
- What interruptions and changes of plan are normal?
- What is repeated and can become habitual?
- Which decisions can be deferred?
- What must remain under user control?
- What errors or delays have serious consequences?

## Novices and experienced users

- Give novices clear entry points, familiar language, safe exploration, and recoverable actions.
- Give frequent users efficient repetition, remembered context, shortcuts, and bulk operations where appropriate.
- Do not split the product into entirely different conceptual models without a strong reason.
- Let users change course as their knowledge or goal changes.
