# Study design

Use this reference to choose a practical method and scope.

## Match the method to the question

Use moderated qualitative usability testing to learn:

- whether people understand the product or screen;
- how they attempt realistic tasks;
- where they hesitate, fail, recover, or form the wrong model;
- why an observed interaction problem occurs;
- whether a changed design addresses an earlier problem.

Use other methods when the question differs:

- interviews or observation for current activities and unmet needs;
- concept testing for value and comprehension;
- surveys for self-reported attitudes across a defined sample;
- analytics for measured behavior at scale;
- experiments for causal comparison with adequate design and sample;
- accessibility testing with relevant users and assistive technologies for lived interaction barriers;
- expert audit for rapid heuristic diagnosis without user evidence.

## Test early and repeatedly

Useful artifacts include:

- competitor or analogous products before design;
- sketches and paper flows;
- wireframes;
- clickable prototypes;
- content drafts;
- existing products before redesign;
- implemented features before and after release.

Match task realism to prototype fidelity. Do not interpret prototype limitations as product findings without labeling them.

## Lean cadence

A recurring small round is usually more useful than one elaborate late study because the team can apply findings and test again. A common do-it-yourself starting point is a few sessions in one block followed by an immediate debrief. Adjust for:

- diversity of roles, abilities, devices, and workflows;
- consequence and safety;
- product complexity;
- whether patterns repeat or new findings continue;
- available time to fix issues before the next round.

“Three users” is a practical cadence, not proof of completeness. Continue or segment research when important variation remains unexplored.

## Moderated versus unmoderated

Choose moderated testing for new, complex, sensitive, exploratory, or diagnostic questions where follow-up matters. Choose unmoderated testing for stable tasks that can be understood without assistance and where the prototype is reliable. Unmoderated recordings still require careful task wording, consent, data handling, and interpretation.

## Ethics and privacy

- Collect only data needed for the study.
- Explain recording, storage, access, retention, incentives, and withdrawal.
- Avoid real sensitive credentials and production consequences.
- Provide test accounts and safe data.
- Make accommodations without treating disability or difficulty as participant failure.
- Do not conceal persuasive or harmful study purposes.
